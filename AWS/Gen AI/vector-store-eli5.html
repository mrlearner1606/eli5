The idea

A computer can't "understand," but it can measure closeness.

Humans read the word "cat" and picture a furry animal. A computer can't picture anything — it only knows numbers.

So we do a clever translation. We turn "cat" into a list of numbers, like [0.82, -0.11, 0.53, ...], and we do the same for "dog," "car," "coffee," and millions of other things.

That list of numbers is an embedding.

When two things mean similar things, their number-lists end up close together in space — just like on the map above, where "cat" and "dog" sit near each other, and "cars" is far away.

🧸 In one breath: Embed each thing into a list of numbers. Things that mean similar things land near each other on the invisible map.

What it really is

Think of it as a translator into "number language."

Every sentence, picture, or document gets its own address in number-space. That address is the embedding.

“A fluffy animal that says meow” human words
[0.82, −0.11, 0.53, …]
← same meaning, now in number form embedding

Now the computer can do something it's actually good at: measure distance. If your question's embedding sits near the "cat" embedding, the computer knows those two are talking about the same kind of thing — even if they share no words.

🧸 Kid version: it's like giving every idea a seat in a giant invisible theater. Similar ideas sit together. The computer just finds the nearest seats.

How it works

From "stuff" to "stuff I can find later" — the pipeline.

01
Gather your things. Documents, photos, notes, products — any collection you want to search through or ask questions about.
⬇
02
Turn each thing into an embedding. An AWS model reads it and writes down its "address" — a long list of numbers that captures what it's about.
⬇
03
Put those number-lists into a vector store. A vector store is a special kind of database built for "find me things near this other thing" — not exact keyword matches, but meaning matches.
⬇
04
Ask a question. Turn the question into an embedding too, then ask the vector store: "what's already here that's closest to this?"
⬇
05
Hand the nearest things to the model. The gen-AI reads the closest matches and uses them to answer you — that's the heart of RAG.

🧸 Plain talk: embed your stuff, stash the number-lists, ask in numbers, get back the closest things. That's the whole trick.

The ingredients

Two pieces working together.

Piece 1 · the translator

Embedding model

The model that reads something and spits out its number-list. In AWS, this is often a model in Amazon Bedrock — the same family of models that powers Gen AI features.

Piece 2 · the filing cabinet

Vector store

The place that holds all the number-lists and knows how to quickly find the ones nearest to a new query. Think "database, but for similarity instead of exact lookup."

Bonus · the question

Your query, embedded too

You don't search with keywords — you search by meaning. Your question becomes an embedding, and the store finds the things that sit near it on the map.

The weird part that makes it work

The map is weird on purpose.

An embedding isn't a neat "category" — it's usually a long list of numbers, sometimes hundreds or thousands of them. That's not because humans need that many numbers to think about "cat." It's because the model gets better at telling subtle things apart when it has more room to spread ideas out.

one embedding ≈ a point in a 1,536-dimensional space · close points = similar meaning

You don't have to picture 1,536 directions. Just remember the smaller version: the model carves up meaning so that related things land near each other and unrelated things drift apart.

🧸 In one breath: more dimensions = finer distinctions. The model gets to draw a richer map.

384 dims · small, fast models 1,536 dims · common Bedrock-style size larger dims · more detail, more space
How the search actually feels

You don't ask "does it contain the word 'cat'?"

Old search matches words. Embeddings + vector stores match meaning. That changes what "finding" feels like.

Keyword search

"Find pages with the word 'kitten'"

Misses "cat," "feline," "purring animal," and anything described without the exact word. Rigid and literal.

Vector search

"Find me things close to 'small furry pet that meows'"

Brings back cat pages even if they never said "kitten." Because the meaning of that phrase sits near the cat embedding.

🧸 Kid version: keyword search is like looking for a specific label on a box. Vector search is like asking a friend who actually read every box and can point you to the ones that feel like what you want.

On AWS

Where embeddings and vector stores live in the AWS Gen AI world.

🧠 Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases

The managed home for this whole pipeline. You bring documents; Bedrock can chunk them, create embeddings with a Bedrock model, store the vectors, and then answer questions by finding the nearest pieces. It's the easiest AWS on-ramp to "make my data searchable by meaning."

🗄️ Vector database options

The actual store can live in a few places AWS touches — for example OpenSearch Service with k-NN, or other vector-friendly stores many teams already use with Bedrock. Bedrock Knowledge Bases can plug into a vector store so the embeddings have somewhere to live and be searched.

🧵 Amazon SageMaker

When you want full control: pick your own embedding model, your own vector store, your own chunking, your own distance style. More work, more flexibility — the "build the pipeline yourself" route.

📦 Amazon Bedrock / foundation models

The same models that power Gen AI can also produce embeddings. That matters because it means the thing that understands your data at query time is the same kind of thing that encoded it in the first place — same world, same "map."

🧸 Plain talk: on AWS, the quick story is Bedrock Knowledge Bases for "I want this to work." SageMaker for "I want to choose every piece." Either way, the idea is the same: embed first, store vectors, search by closeness.
